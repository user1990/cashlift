Lesson 04 · Signal

Coverage is a map, not proof

Coverage shows where tests travelled. It does not show whether they noticed the scenery—or whether the map contains the right destination.

Coverage kindsCyclomatic complexityCRAP metric

Win for this lesson

You can read coverage and complexity together to choose where to investigate, without treating either score as a certificate of correctness.

What each signal can say

SignalIt can revealIt cannot prove
Statement coverageWhich executable statements ranThat results were asserted correctly
Branch coverageWhich decision outcomes ranThat all important input classes exist
Cyclomatic complexitySize of a basis set of independent control-flow pathsBusiness risk, readability, or total possible paths
CRAPWhere complexity and weak coverage coincideThat low-scoring code is well designed

Precision matters

Cyclomatic complexity is often described loosely as “the number of paths.” More precisely, it counts a basis set of linearly independent paths under a tool’s control-flow model. Boolean expressions, exceptions, and switch statements may be counted differently by different tools.

The original CRAP1 formula

CRAP(m) = comp(m)² × (1 − coverage(m) / 100)³ + comp(m)

The score rises sharply when a method is both complex and poorly covered. At 100% coverage, the first term becomes zero, so CRAP equals cyclomatic complexity. At 0% coverage, it becomes comp² + comp.

Two threshold stories

The original CRAP1 author proposed over 30 as a warning heuristic. In the supplied interview, Robert Martin describes personal experiments keeping human code below 4 and agent-oriented code near 6 or 8. Those are not standards. Tool counting rules and coverage types vary, so calibrate against defects and review burden in your repository.
